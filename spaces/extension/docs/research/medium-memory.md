# Medium and memory: psychology research for Polycarp

Prepared 2026-11-20 for the Polycarp reading extension (re-rendering, read-aloud with word sync, recall checkpoints, spaced review).

## How to read this file

Verification status, applied to every citation:

- VERIFIED: bibliographic details (authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages or DOI) appeared in a search result this session.
- SECONDARY: the effect size or finding came from a search excerpt or from a later paper that cites the work. The original was not read.
- UNVERIFIED: not found this session. Cited from memory or from a secondary mention only. Check before quoting.

Evidence ratings used in the design rules:

- STRONG: several meta-analyses or large replicated sets agree.
- MODERATE: one or two meta-analyses, or consistent lab work, with known moderators.
- WEAK: small effects, few studies, mixed replications, or only secondary support.
- AGAINST: the evidence argues against the practice or claim.

Method note: the academic index tool returned no results for PubMed, Crossref, OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar during this pass. Verification used web search excerpts. No full text was read. Effect sizes marked SECONDARY should be checked against the original paper before they appear in any public copy.

### Corrections to the brief and to popular belief

- Mazza et al. (2016) appeared in *Psychological Science*, not *Current Biology*. No "pre-sleep review" version was found. [MAZ16]
- Clinton-Lisell's 2022 meta-analysis is in *Review of Educational Research*, not *Educational Research Review* or *Journal of Research in Reading*. [CLL22]
- Ackerman and Goldsmith (2011) is in *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, not *Learning, Memory, and Cognition*. [ACK11]
- Rogowsky, Calhoun and Tallal is 2015 (*Journal of Educational Psychology* 107), not 2016. [ROG15]
- Ponce et al. (2022) has Ester E. Méndez as third author, not "Loyola." [PON22]
- The 2019 Kindle study is by Mangen, Olivier and Velay, not "Gorsky." [MAN19]
- No paper titled "Eidetic images are not photographic" was found. The closest match is Haber's 1979 target article. [HAB79]
- Twomey and Kroneisen's method-of-loci meta-analysis could not be located. Its journal and year are UNVERIFIED. [TWO21]
- Popular belief that people read faster on screens is not supported. Clinton (2019) found no reading-time difference. [CLI19]
- Popular belief that "photographic memory" is a real, trainable capability is not supported. See section 8. [HAB79]
- The "shallowing hypothesis" is a popular idea from Carr's book *The Shallows* (UNVERIFIED citation). It is not a tested model in the meta-analyses below. [CAR10]

## 1. Paper vs screen comprehension

Strongest findings:

- Delgado et al. [DEL18] pooled comparisons of paper and digital reading. Overall effect was small and favored paper: Hedges' g of about -.21 (SECONDARY). The gap was larger under time pressure (about -.26, SECONDARY).
- Clinton [CLI19] found a small penalty for screen reading on performance, g of about -.25 (SECONDARY), random-effects. Readers' judgments of their own performance were more accurate on paper, about g = .20 (SECONDARY). The penalty appeared for expository text. Narrative text showed no significant paper advantage (SECONDARY). No reading-time difference between formats was found (SECONDARY).
- Kong, Seo and Zhai [KON18] pooled 17 studies (SECONDARY). Comprehension favored paper. Reading speed did not differ significantly. The comprehension gap appeared to narrow over the study window (SECONDARY).
- Singer and Alexander [SIN17] screened 878 studies and kept 36 direct comparisons with reliable learning measures (SECONDARY). Longer texts were better understood in print. Format mattered less for superficial reading and very young readers (SECONDARY).
- Ackerman and Goldsmith [ACK11] show the gap is partly metacognitive. Screen learners predicted their performance less accurately and regulated study time less steadily (SECONDARY). Later work reports that the screen deficit appeared under time pressure and disappeared under a limited time frame for some students (SECONDARY). Ackerman and Lauterman [ACK12] replicated the time-pressure dependence (title VERIFIED, details SECONDARY).

Replication status: the direction (paper slightly ahead for comprehension) replicates across reviews. The size is small. Moderators are expository text, time pressure, and calibration. Handheld-device studies are a smaller subset with a smaller effect (about g = -.11, SECONDARY, Salmerón and colleagues).

Popular belief: "screens are fine for light reading." The narrative-text finding supports this in part. The belief that people skim screens faster is not supported by Clinton's reading-time data.

Design implications: the penalty is concentrated where a reader is expected to learn expository material under time pressure. Polycarp should (a) never add a hidden timer, (b) show calibration and progress, and (c) keep paper-like affordances such as highlight and margin notes (see section 4).

## 2. Scrolling vs pagination and spatial layout

- Rothkopf [ROT71] reported that readers encode where information sat in a text without being asked to (SECONDARY). Later papers cite this and two related studies, Lovelace and Southall [LOV83] and Zechmeister et al. (UNVERIFIED), for the same incidental-location effect.
- Mangen, Olivier and Velay [MAN19] compared a long text read on a print book and on a Kindle (SECONDARY). Overall comprehension was similar. The clearest difference was on chronology and where events sat in the text: print readers did better. The authors attribute this to weaker physical cues on the Kindle.
- A press report describes a study of 50 readers with a short story on Kindle or paper, where Kindle readers did worse at ordering 14 plot events (PRESS ONLY, UNVERIFIED). It may or may not be the same study as [MAN19].
- Mangen et al. (2013) is cited as a paper-advantage study (UNVERIFIED).

Replication status: the location-memory effect is consistent with the cognitive-map account, but this session did not find a modern meta-analysis of spatial memory in digital reading. Treat as WEAK.

Design implications: show a visible position bar, section numbers, and an optional page map (a thumbnail of the whole text with the reader's position marked). Pagination is not required; a stable map is what the spatial evidence supports.

## 3. Audio, bimodal reading, and text-to-speech

- Clinton-Lisell's 2022 meta-analysis of 46 studies (N = 4,687) [CLL22] found no reliable overall difference between reading and listening comprehension (g = 0.07, n.s., SECONDARY). Self-paced reading favored reading (g = 0.13, SECONDARY). Experimenter-paced reading did not (SECONDARY). Reading had an edge on inferential questions (g = 0.36, SECONDARY), not literal ones.
- Clinton-Lisell's 2023 review of reading while listening [CLL23] found a trivial overall benefit of reading while listening over reading alone (g = .18, SECONDARY). The benefit appeared only where the experimenter paced the text (SECONDARY).
- Rogowsky, Calhoun and Tallal [ROG15] tested the meshing hypothesis: matching instruction to a learner's auditory or visual preference. No support was found in adults (SECONDARY). Rogowsky et al. [ROG20] found the same in fifth graders, where visual learners outperformed auditory learners on both listening and reading tests (SECONDARY). Popular belief that learning styles should drive format choice is not supported.
- Wood et al. [WOO18] meta-analyzed 22 group studies (1995-2014) of text-to-speech and read-aloud for students with reading disabilities: d = .35, 95% CI .14 to .56 (SECONDARY). The authors called for more moderator studies.
- Dyslexia: a 2022 meta-analysis (Annals of Dyslexia, 76 studies) reports weaker listening comprehension than age peers, but a smaller gap than in reading comprehension (SECONDARY, authors UNVERIFIED).
- Reading while listening with word-level sync: a separate review (EPR, DOI 10.1007/s10648-021-09653-2, authors UNVERIFIED) reports that hearing audio alongside print improved comprehension for struggling readers and EFL learners more than print alone (SECONDARY).
- ADHD: no sourced study found this pass. GAP.
- Second-language learners: partial support only, via the EPR review above. GAP for effect sizes.
- Speech rate: Foulke and Sticht [FOU69] reported that comprehension falls at a moderate rate as word rate rises from about 175 to 275 words per minute, and faster above that (SECONDARY). Heiman et al. [HEI86] argued that word-level intelligibility loss explains more of the decline than the earlier review allowed (SECONDARY).
- Voice quality and naturalness: no sourced effect size found this pass. GAP. Treat any claim about voice naturalness boosting engagement as UNVERIFIED.

Replication status: reading-while-listening has a small benefit for students with reading disabilities and for experimenter-paced conditions. Modality matching is not supported.

Design implications: offer word-sync read-along as an option, default off for general readers. Default text-to-speech in the 175-250 words per minute range. Do not sort readers into "auditory" or "visual" modes.

## 4. Highlighting, underlining, rereading, notes, and generative learning

- Dunlosky et al. [DUN13] rated ten techniques. Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility. Summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, and rereading received low utility (SECONDARY, consistent across sources).
- Ponce, Mayer and Méndez [PON22] meta-analyzed 36 articles (85 effect sizes) (SECONDARY). Learner-generated highlighting improved memory (about 0.36, SECONDARY) but comprehension only weakly (about 0.20, SECONDARY). Instructor-provided highlighting improved both memory and comprehension (about 0.44, SECONDARY). Students benefited more from highlights supplied for them than from their own.
- Rereading previously highlighted text did not significantly beat reading or studying once (SECONDARY, commentary on [PON22]).
- Fiorella and Mayer [FIO16] reviewed eight generative strategies: summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, and enacting (VERIFIED list via abstract). Generative activity works when the prompt directs the learner to organize and connect material. Summarizing is listed by Fiorella and Mayer but rated low by Dunlosky, so the strategy works only when guided.
- Self-explanation (Chi et al., 1994) is a well-known generative strategy. Not verified this pass. UNVERIFIED.

Design implications: highlights and notes should be persistent and visible, but the core loop should not be highlighting or rereading. Offer an optional instructor-style key-sentence layer. Pair each section with a generative prompt, not a summary request.

## 5. Retrieval practice and the testing effect

- Roediger and Karpicke [ROE06] (VERIFIED) used passages on two topics across two experiments. Repeated testing without feedback improved delayed retention over restudy, even though restudy raised confidence (SECONDARY). Restudy won on the 5-minute test.
- Rowland [ROW14] meta-analyzed testing against restudy. The reported effect was Hedges' g of about 0.50 (SECONDARY).
- Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan [ADE17] found practice tests beat restudy and other comparisons (SECONDARY). Feedback added no reliable benefit in some included studies. The testing effect appeared with and without feedback, per a later review (SECONDARY). Multiple-choice tests were at least as effective as free and cued recall for producing testing effects (SECONDARY). Sample counts conflict across secondary sources (118 articles vs 217 studies). Check the original.
- Generation effect: Slamecka and Graf [SLA78] (VERIFIED) showed that generating a target from a cue (for example, "fast" from "rapid-f") produced better later memory than reading the target (SECONDARY). Bertsch et al. [BER07] pooled 445 effect sizes from 86 studies (SECONDARY) and reported a typical advantage of about 9% (about .40 SD, SECONDARY). Free recall showed the smallest effect (SECONDARY). McCurdy et al. [MCC20] found similar patterns in a larger follow-up (SECONDARY).
- Hirshman and Bjork [HIR88] complicated effort and depth explanations of generation (SECONDARY).

Replication status: testing effect is among the most replicated results in learning science. The feedback moderator is mixed. Multiple-choice counts as retrieval, but free and cued recall may build stronger memory.

Design implications: after each section, ask for recall before showing the next section. Use free or cued recall for the review queue when practical. Show correct answers right after each attempt. Do not build feedback beyond the answer and a short explanation until tested.

## 6. Spacing and successive relearning

- Cepeda et al. [CEP06] synthesized 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles (SECONDARY). The spacing advantage was large and held across verbal recall tasks. The best gap depends on the retention interval (SECONDARY).
- Cepeda et al. [CEP08] found that the best gap between study sessions is nonmonotonic. Retention rises as the gap grows, then falls. For a one-week test, the best gap was about 20-40% of the test delay (SECONDARY). When the delay was longer, the optimal gap increased in absolute terms but shrank as a proportion. The study used delays up to one year (SECONDARY).
- Cepeda et al. [CEP09] extended the analysis to multi-day intervals (VERIFIED bibliography, findings SECONDARY).
- Rawson and Dunlosky [RAW11] proposed successive relearning: retrieve, then relearn, across separated sessions until items reach criterion. This gave durable retention with efficient effort (SECONDARY). The DOI is UNVERIFIED.
- Expanding versus uniform intervals: Landauer and Bjork [LAN78] and Karpicke and Roediger [KAR07] are cited from memory (UNVERIFIED). The expanding-versus-equal-spacing result is contested in the literature.
- Schedulers: FSRS (Ye, 2022, per secondary sources) is reported to predict recall better than SM-2 in an open benchmark of 9,999 Anki collections, with lower log loss in 99.6% of them (SECONDARY). Anki's own documentation calls the comparison preliminary (SECONDARY). SM-2's creator, Piotr Woźniak, disputes the metrics (SECONDARY). The benchmark is retrospective, not a randomized trial. Leitner systems are classic and used widely; no evidence was checked this pass (UNVERIFIED).

Design implications: spacing is the single strongest scheduling lever. Start review gaps at about 20-40% of the delay to the next test. Keep items in review until they are recalled across separate sessions. Ship a published scheduler and tune it on your own logs.

## 7. Pretesting and prequestions

- Richland, Kornell and Kao [RIC09] (VERIFIED) showed that pretesting with open-ended questions before reading improved final test scores over extra study time (SECONDARY). The analysis covered only items the pretest had not answered correctly (SECONDARY). Five experiments used a short text (SECONDARY).
- Carpenter and Toftness [CAR17] had 85 students watch a short video on Easter Island. Prequestions improved retention, including material not asked about (SECONDARY).
- Toftness, Carpenter, Lauber and Mickes [TOF18] found limited effects of prequestions on authentic lecture videos (title VERIFIED, findings SECONDARY). Their point: the 2017 result used two simple prequestions and a two-minute clip.
- St. Hilaire and Carpenter [STH20] (2019 APS poster, SECONDARY): benefit for video lectures appeared only when the same questions were asked on the pretest and posttest. Full paper UNVERIFIED.
- Advance organizers and heading previews: Ausubel-style organizers are a classic idea. Not checked this pass. UNVERIFIED.

Replication status: pretesting is a reliable effect in short texts. Effects on long authentic lectures are small and depend on whether the same questions reappear.

Design implications: before a section, show two or three questions the reader will be able to answer at the end. Answers are shown after the reading, not before. Expect a small effect on long material.

## 8. Elaboration, dual coding, mnemonics, and eidetic memory

- Clark and Paivio [CLK91] present dual coding theory as a framework linking verbal and image codes in education (VERIFIED bibliography, page range disputed). The theory is a framework. Its educational predictions have stronger support when the visual and verbal forms are aligned and when the images carry the same content. Dual-coding claims should not be stated as settled without the original.
- Dunlosky et al. [DUN13] rated the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text learning low utility (SECONDARY). Their concern: students often fail to use them well without training.
- Method of loci: Dresler et al. [DRE17] (VERIFIED) trained 51 naive volunteers for six weeks. The method-of-loci group improved more than active and passive controls (SECONDARY). Similarity of their brain connectivity to memory athletes predicted gains up to four months later (SECONDARY). The study measured mnemonic memory, not general intelligence.
- Ondřej [OND25] (SECONDARY) is a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of the method of loci. It cites the earlier Twomey and Kroneisen meta-analysis [TWO21] (UNVERIFIED).
- Eidetic memory: Haber [HAB79] (pages VERIFIED) reviewed twenty years of eidetic imagery research (SECONDARY). Eidetic imagery is rare, found mostly in young children and essentially absent in adults (SECONDARY). Haber found little or no consistent link between eidetic imagery and cognitive, neurological, or emotional measures (SECONDARY). Some investigators treat eidetic imagery as a distinct phenomenon. Others argue it is an unusually vivid form of ordinary visual memory (SECONDARY). Eidetic recall is neither perfect nor permanent (SECONDARY).

Honest answer on "photographic memory": no research supports a tool that gives adults a photographic memory. Measurable gains come from structured practice: retrieval, spacing, generation, and mnemonic training over weeks (see [DRE17], [ROE06], [CEP06]). Polycarp should not promise or market photographic recall.

## 9. Sleep and review timing

- Mazza et al. [MAZ16] (title VERIFIED, DOI VERIFIED) compared relearning after a 12-hour interval that included sleep or waking. The sleep group needed about 3 attempts to reach mastery, the wake group about 6 (SECONDARY). A week later, the sleep group recalled more (SECONDARY). This was a 16-pair Swahili-French associate study, not a Current Biology paper.
- A 2020 replication in *Sleep* reported no relearning advantage from prior sleep, and a reduced consolidation advantage one week later (SECONDARY, authors UNVERIFIED).

Replication status: WEAK. One positive result and one failed replication with a similar design.

Design implications: a short evening review of items due the next morning is cheap and harmless. Do not design the schedule around a sleep effect. The scheduler's spacing matters more (section 6).

## 10. Chunking, segmenting, breaks, and attention

- Segmenting principle (Mayer and Chandler [MAY01], UNVERIFIED): presenting lessons in learner-paced segments improves learning from multimedia. Not verified this pass.
- Ariga and Lleras [ARI11] (VERIFIED bibliography) found that brief, sporadic retrieval tasks during a long vigilance task avoided the performance decline seen in a group that did the retrieval only at the end (SECONDARY). The authors attribute the decline to goal habituation, not depletion (SECONDARY). Sources disagree on the task length (40 or 50 minutes, SECONDARY).
- Vigilance decrement: Mackworth (1948) [MAC48] is the classic study (UNVERIFIED).
- Pomodoro: no rigorous evidence of a productivity advantage over self-chosen breaks was found. Biwer et al. [BIW23] (SECONDARY) compared fixed breaks, Pomodoro-style blocks and self-chosen breaks among 87 students. Fixed breaks helped mood and efficiency (SECONDARY). A blog-cited 94-student study reported no productivity difference (UNVERIFIED). A single-person randomized self-experiment exists (not evidence). Popular claims of "25% productivity boosts" trace to sources that could not be found (UNVERIFIED).

Replication status: breaks help sustained attention in vigilance-type tasks. The 25-5 timing has no special support.

Design implications: suggest breaks at natural section boundaries. Let the reader choose the length. Do not hard-code Pomodoro timing.

## 11. Motivation and habit

- Sailer and Homner [SAI20] (VERIFIED bibliography) pooled gamification studies: cognitive outcomes g = .49 (k = 19) and motivational outcomes g = .36 (k = 16) (SECONDARY). The cognitive effect held in a high-rigor subset. The motivational effect did not (SECONDARY). The "motivational" category was broad: it included attitudes, engagement, and self-efficacy, not only intrinsic motivation (SECONDARY).
- Deci, Koestner and Ryan [DEC99] (VERIFIED) meta-analyzed 128 experiments. Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation, with effect sizes of about d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28 (SECONDARY). The finding is disputed: Cameron and Pierce [CAM94] (UNVERIFIED) concluded the undermining effect was minimal. The disagreement is open. Treat the undermining claim as MODERATE, not settled.
- Mol and Bus [MOL11] pooled 99 studies (N = 7,669) on leisure reading (SECONDARY). Print exposure correlated moderately to strongly with reading comprehension and related skills. The authors describe a reciprocal "upward spiral" where better readers read more (SECONDARY). Correlational, not causal.
- Streaks: an INSEAD study [INS] (authors UNVERIFIED) found users were more likely to keep acting when an app showed an intact streak than a broken one, even when behavior was the same (SECONDARY). The effect shrank when repair was possible and grew when users felt personally responsible for a break (SECONDARY). Critiques argue users come to value the streak more than the activity (Decision Lab summary, UNVERIFIED). No published controlled study tests streaks against learning outcomes was found this pass.
- Loss aversion: Kahneman and Tversky [KAH79] (UNVERIFIED). Duolingo's blog cites loss aversion for its streak. That is a company claim, not evidence.
- Variable rewards: Ferster and Skinner [FER57] (UNVERIFIED) on variable-ratio schedules. No reading-app study found this pass.

Replication status: gamification has small, uneven effects. Tangible, contingent rewards are negative or contested. Streak evidence is lab-based, not learning-outcome evidence.

Design implications: reward competence and progress (what the reader can now do), not time spent. Avoid pay-to-repair streaks, guilt copy, and loss framing. If Polycarp uses a streak, make it a visible record that the reader can ignore, and measure whether learning improves, not just return visits. Track print-like volume (minutes read, books finished) as an outcome, not a claim the app causes reading growth.

## 12. Reading speed claims

- Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman [RAY16] (VERIFIED bibliography) concluded that doubling or tripling reading speed without losing comprehension is improbable (SECONDARY).
- Typical normal reading is about 250 words per minute (SECONDARY). Skilled readers average 200-400 words per minute (SECONDARY).
- Eye movements account for no more than about 10% of total reading time (SECONDARY). Tools that remove eye movements address a small share of time.
- Trained speed readers show reduced comprehension as speed rises (SECONDARY).
- Speed-reading programs may teach skimming. Skimming is useful when thorough understanding is not the goal (SECONDARY).

Replication status: this is a review and position paper, not a single experiment. The claim against speed reading without loss is consistent with the broader literature. Treat as STRONG against the claim.

Design implications: do not promise doubled reading speed. Show estimated time and let readers choose skim or study modes. Measure comprehension at higher speeds before claiming any gain.

## Design rules for Polycarp (medium, audio, memory, motivation)

1. Screen penalty is small (about g = -.2 to -.25) and concentrated in expository text under time pressure: make the screen reader carry paper-like affordances instead of avoiding screens. MODERATE [CLI19, DEL18].
2. No hidden timers; show elapsed time and only announced limits. MODERATE [ACK11, DEL18].
3. After each recall check, show predicted versus actual performance, because the screen gap is partly metacognitive. MODERATE [ACK11, CLI19].
4. Keep a stable page or section map with a visible position marker; location memory is incidental and print advantages appear on temporal and location questions. WEAK [ROT71, MAN19].
5. Keep learner highlights and margin notes persistent; they help memory more than comprehension. MODERATE for memory, WEAK for comprehension [PON22].
6. Offer an optional key-sentence layer; instructor-supplied highlights beat learner highlights. MODERATE [PON22].
7. Do not make rereading or highlighting the core study loop; both are rated low utility. STRONG [DUN13].
8. Ask for recall after each section before showing the next one. STRONG [DUN13, ROE06, ADE17].
9. Multiple-choice counts as retrieval, but use free or cued recall for the review queue when practical. MODERATE [ADE17].
10. Show two or three pre-questions before a section; expect small effects on long authentic lectures. MODERATE for short text, WEAK for long video [RIC09, CAR17, TOF18].
11. Use generation prompts (fill the blank before reveal, one-line self-explanation after). MODERATE [BER07, SLA78, FIO16].
12. Space reviews; the gap between sessions should grow as the test delay grows. STRONG [CEP06].
13. Start review gaps at about 20-40% of the delay to the next test. MODERATE [CEP08].
14. An item is mastered only after recall across separated sessions (successive relearning). MODERATE [RAW11].
15. Ship a published scheduler (FSRS, or SM-2 as fallback) and tune it on your own logs; the benchmarks are retrospective. WEAK [FSR].
16. Offer an optional evening review for items due next morning; do not design around the sleep effect. WEAK [MAZ16, MAZ-R].
17. Split long texts into labeled sections with a progress bar, and suggest short breaks at section boundaries instead of fixed Pomodoro timing. WEAK [MAY01 UNVERIFIED, ARI11, BIW23].
18. Offer word-synced read-along audio, default off for general readers; it helps students with reading disabilities (d = .35). MODERATE for reading disabilities, WEAK for general readers [WOO18, CLL23].
19. Default text-to-speech speed in the 175-250 words per minute range; comprehension falls faster above about 275. MODERATE [FOU69].
20. Do not match the format to a "learning style." AGAINST [ROG15, ROG20].
21. Do not promise doubled reading speed or speed reading without loss; offer skim and study modes instead. AGAINST [RAY16].
22. Do not market or promise "photographic memory." Eidetic imagery is rare and not photographic, and mnemonic gains take weeks of training. AGAINST [HAB79, DRE17].
23. Reward competence and progress, not time spent or points for reading; tangible contingent rewards may undermine intrinsic motivation, though this is disputed. MODERATE [DEC99, SAI20, CAM94].
24. If you use streaks, drop guilt copy and paid repair; measure learning, not just return visits. WEAK [INS].
25. Track reading volume (minutes read, books finished) as an outcome, not a causal promise: volume correlates with comprehension, and the link runs both ways. MODERATE (correlational) [MOL11].
